# Supermarket Product Placement Optimisation

> Probabilistic simulation of customer purchasing behaviour and supermarket layout optimisation.

## Overview

This project investigates how the spatial organisation of products in a supermarket can influence customer purchasing behaviour and the resulting revenue.

The project was developed as part of a French scientific research project (TIPE).

The approach combines probability, simulation and optimisation. A simplified supermarket is modelled as a grid of departments, and customer behaviour is simulated using assumed purchasing probabilities and product relationships.

Different supermarket layouts are then compared according to the average revenue generated per customer.

## Objective

The objective is to determine whether the spatial arrangement of supermarket departments can influence customer purchases and to identify layouts that maximise expected revenue.

Three types of layouts are studied:

- random organisation;
- organisation placing strongly associated departments close together;
- organisation placing strongly associated departments further apart.

## Data and assumptions

No real customer transaction data were used in this project as they are confidential and not available to the public. However, should a real supermarket want to implement the same algorithms, they could use their customer data.  

Instead, the model was constructed using publicly available information and manually defined assumptions designed to represent plausible purchasing behaviour.

Three main datasets are used:

### Initial shopping-list probabilities

Each product is assigned a probability representing the likelihood that it is included in a customer's initial shopping list.

These probabilities are used to generate simulated shopping lists.

### Product relationship matrix

A matrix assigns a numerical value to each pair of products, representing the assumed strength of their purchasing relationship.

These relationships are used to model how encountering products during a customer's journey can influence additional purchases.

### Product prices

Each product is assigned an average price. These prices are used to calculate the total value of each simulated customer's purchases and therefore evaluate the revenue generated by each supermarket layout.

The datasets used in the simulation are model assumptions rather than empirical measurements of real customer behaviour.

## Methodology

### 1. Modelling the supermarket

The supermarket is represented as a 4 × 4 grid.

Each cell corresponds to a department, with the entrance and checkout represented by specific locations.

The model contains 15 departments and 44 products distributed among them.

### 2. Generating shopping lists

For each simulated customer, an initial shopping list is generated probabilistically.

Each product has an associated probability of being included in the list.

A random draw is performed for each product, creating a different shopping list for each simulated customer while preserving the assumed purchasing frequencies.

### 3. Modelling departments relationships

The product relationship matrix is used to represent the assumed relationships between departments.

These relationships are incorporated into the simulation to determine the probability of purchasing products in one department knowing that a product was bought in another one during the customer's journey through the supermarket.

### 4. Simulating customer movement

Customers move through the supermarket on a 4 × 4 grid.

Movement is restricted to horizontal and vertical directions, with no diagonal movement.

For each target department, a Breadth-First Search (BFS) algorithm is used to determine a shortest path from the customer's current position.

The departments crossed during this path are then considered as potential opportunities for additional purchases.

### 5. Layout strategies

Three different layout strategies are compared.

#### Random layout

Departments are placed randomly within the supermarket.

#### Proximity-based layout

Departments with stronger purchasing relationships are placed close to one another.

#### Distance-based layout

Departments with stronger purchasing relationships are deliberately placed further apart.

### 6. Revenue evaluation

For each simulated customer, the total value of the purchased products is calculated using the assigned average product prices.

Each layout is evaluated over multiple simulated customers.

The average spending per customer is then used as the main performance criterion.

The objective is therefore to maximise:

$$
\text{Expected revenue per customer}
$$

## Results

The simulation produced a counter-intuitive result: in the current model, the random layout generally generated higher revenue than the two probability-based layouts.

This result suggests that the assumptions used in the model have a significant influence on the outcome.

Several limitations were identified, including:

- the simplified model of customer movement;
- the assumptions used to construct the purchasing probabilities;
- the limited representation of real supermarket behaviour.

These limitations provide potential directions for improving the model.
